Inside BM25

Every search box has to answer one question: of the documents that contain your words, which one did you actually mean? BM25 answers it with three ideas and a little arithmetic. This walks through all of it — using the real index built over the 547 emails in data/emails.json.

The shape of the problem

Search for mortgage pre-approval and 28 of our emails contain the word "mortgage". Returning all 28 in the order they happen to sit on disk is not search; it is a filter. Search means ordering — and to order, you need a number per document that says how well it answers the query.

BM25 produces that number. The full formula looks like this, and if it means nothing to you yet, that's the point — we'll build it from scratch, one idea at a time, and come back to it.

score(d) = Σt ∈ query  idf(t) · tf̃(t,d) · (k₁ + 1) / (tf̃(t,d) + k₁)
where  tf̃(t,d) = Σf ∈ fields wf · tf(t, d.f) / (1 − b + b · len(d.f) / avgdlf)

Three colours, three ideas. How rare is this word? How often does it appear here? How long is this document? Everything else is scaffolding.

Step zero: text becomes terms

Before any scoring happens, both the documents and the query have to be cut into comparable pieces. This is the tokenizer, and it is the single most consequential piece of a search engine — a term that the tokenizer discards can never be searched for, no matter how good the ranking maths is.

Ours does four things, plus one rule specific to email.

Consequence

Because the tokenizer runs on the query too, matching is exact-token, not substring. Typing mortg matches nothing. This is the single biggest behavioural difference from the String.includes filter this replaced, and it is a deliberate v1 trade: prefix matching is a listed non-goal.

Idea one: repetition counts, but less and less

The oldest idea in retrieval is term frequency: an email that says "mortgage" five times is probably more about mortgages than one that says it once. True. But is it five times more about mortgages? Obviously not. The second mention tells you far less than the first, and the twentieth tells you almost nothing — by then you already knew.

So raw tf is wrong. What we want is a curve that rises quickly at first and then flattens — saturates. BM25 gets it with a piece of arithmetic that does exactly that, controlled by a knob called k₁:

tf · (k₁ + 1) / (tf + k₁)   ← approaches k₁ + 1 and never exceeds it

Drag k₁ below and watch what it controls. At k₁ = 0 the curve collapses into a step: any occurrence at all scores the same, and repetition is ignored entirely. Crank it up and the curve straightens toward raw counting.

Idea two: rare words carry the meaning

Now the opposite question. The query mortgage pre-approval has three terms after tokenizing. Should they all count equally?

Here is where our real corpus makes the point better than any invented example. These are the terms that appear in the most emails, straight out of the index:

The formula that produces those numbers:

idf(t) = ln( 1 + (N − df(t) + 0.5) / (df(t) + 0.5) )
N = 547 documents  ·  df(t) = how many contain t

The 1 + is not cosmetic. The textbook version of IDF omits it, and goes negative for any term appearing in more than half the corpus — meaning a document could be penalised for containing one of your query words. On a 547-document personal mailbox, where sarah, chen and com all hit every single document, that isn't a rare edge case; it's Tuesday. We use the Lucene variant, which stays positive forever.

Why stopwords are still worth it

IDF already crushes common words, so you might think a stopword list is redundant. Nearly — but a stopword is dropped at index time, so it costs no memory and no postings-list walk at query time. The trade is that a query of pure stopwords ("the and of") has nothing left to match and returns zero results.

Idea three: long documents cheat

Two emails both mention "solicitor" twice. One is a four-line note; the other is the 756-token monster at the end of our corpus. The short one is clearly more about solicitors — the long one mentions everything twice, simply by being long.

Our corpus has exactly this spread. Bodies run from 3 tokens to 756, averaging 52.7:

The fix is to divide tf by how long the document is, relative to average — but only partially, because length isn't purely noise. A long email really might cover more ground. The knob b sets how much of the correction to apply:

tf / (1 − b + b · len(d) / avgdl)
b = 0 → no correction at all  ·  b = 1 → fully proportional to length

Assembling it

That's all three. Multiply the rarity of the term by the saturated, length-corrected frequency of it, and add up across the query:

score(d) = Σt  idf(t) · saturate(normalise(tf))

Terms combine as a plain sum, which makes this an OR: a document matching two of your three words still scores, it just scores less. There is no AND, no phrase matching, and no notion of the words being near each other. "Mortgage" in the subject and "approval" 400 words later in the body counts exactly the same as the phrase sitting intact.

BM25F: not all fields are equal

An email isn't a bag of text; it's a subject, a body, a sender and a recipient. A word in the subject line is a much stronger signal than the same word buried in a quoted reply chain. Plain BM25 can't express that. BM25F — the F is for fields — can, by giving each field a weight:

The one subtle decision

Where the numbers live

None of this would be usable if scoring meant reading 547 emails per keystroke. It doesn't, because of the data structure underneath: an inverted index. Instead of document → words, it stores word → documents.

A score, computed by hand

Abstractions are easy to nod along to, so here is every operation on a three-document corpus small enough to verify with a calculator. This is the exact corpus from bm25.test.ts, and the assertions there check these numbers to six decimal places.

  1. Rarity of the term. It's in 2 of 3 documents, so it isn't worth much — but the Lucene variant keeps it safely positive.
    idf = ln(1 + (3 − 2 + 0.5) / (2 + 0.5)) = ln(1.6) = 0.470004
  2. Length correction, per field, for d1. Its subject is exactly average length so nothing changes; its body is 2 tokens against an average of 1.667, so it's penalised slightly.
    subject: 1 − 0.75 + 0.75 · (1 / 1.000) = 1.000
    body:    1 − 0.75 + 0.75 · (2 / 1.667) = 1.150
  3. Weighted frequency for d1 — field weight × tf ÷ correction, summed across fields before any saturation.
    tf̃ = (3 × 1 / 1.000) + (1 × 1 / 1.150) = 3.000 + 0.870 = 3.869565
  4. Saturate, and scale by rarity.
    score(d1) = 0.470004 × 3.869565 × 2.2 / (3.869565 + 1.2) = 0.789251
  5. Now d2, which has the term only in its body — but in a body that is shorter than average, so normalisation works in its favour.
    body: 1 − 0.75 + 0.75 · (1 / 1.667) = 0.700
    tf̃ = 1 × 1 / 0.700 = 1.428571
    score(d2) = 0.470004 × 1.428571 × 2.2 / (1.428571 + 1.2) = 0.561961
  6. d3 never enters the calculation. It has no posting for "mortgage", so it contributes nothing and is dropped rather than scored as zero.
    → [ d1  0.789251,   d2  0.561961 ]

d1 wins by 40%, and you can point at exactly where the win came from. Of d1's weighted frequency of 3.87, a full 3.00 is the single subject hit — the body mention adds only 0.87. d2's lone body mention, even with the short-document bonus pushing it to 1.43, cannot make that up. The subject weight is doing nearly all the work.

What it cannot do

BM25 is a lexical ranker. It matches the words you typed, weighted cleverly — and that is the entire extent of its understanding.

  • No synonyms or meaning. "Solicitor" will not find "conveyancer". "Car" will not find "automobile". Nothing in the formula knows what words mean.
  • No phrases or proximity. "Pre-approval" is two independent terms; a document mentioning them 400 words apart scores identically to one with the phrase intact.
  • No prefixes or fuzziness. A typo matches nothing at all.
  • No stemming. "Meeting" and "meetings" are unrelated terms. A deliberate call: every JS stemmer is either a dependency or a 200-line transcription, and it costs recall in confusing ways. Revisit if evals show plural misses.
  • No filters. No date ranges, no arcId. Those belong in the agent-tool wrapper that knows what query language it wants to expose.

The first two limits are exactly what a semantic embedding index is good at, and the reverse is also true — embeddings are famously bad at exact identifiers, names and rare terms, which is precisely where BM25 is strongest. That complementarity is the reason this was built as a standalone, corpus-agnostic ranker: it is the lexical half of a hybrid search, waiting to be joined to an embedding half by reciprocal rank fusion.
